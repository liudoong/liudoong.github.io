AI Cooling as a Macro Stress Scenario: Impulse Responses to a 25% Repricing of AI Equities

AI冷却的宏观压力测试：AI相关股票下跌25%的冲击响应分析

✍ Dong Liu 📅 November 2026 💬 Comments welcome
Draft status. Unlike the estimated modules of the regime-switching model, every parameter in this note is illustrative: each is set inside the range of standard estimates, but none is estimated. The note is about the structure of the transmission and the relative size of the channels, not a forecast. Macro context is as of 6 November 2026. All numbers come from the Appendix code.

Abstract

AI investment now accounts for roughly a third of U.S. GDP growth, while the jobs it creates directly are few. That makes the aggregate data a blend of two economies, and it raises a stress-testing question: if AI expectations cool and AI equities reprice, how does the shock travel to the labour market, and what decides its size? This note writes the transmission as a small system (valuation, AI capex, wealth effect, credit spreads, policy rule, labour demand), solves the static version for a closed-form multiplier and loop gain, and then runs a quarterly version under a 25% repricing of AI-linked equities in two policy regimes: a Fed that can cut, and a Fed pinned by energy inflation.

With the Fed free to respond, the output gap bottoms at −1.05% of GDP in Q4 and unemployment peaks 0.39 pp above baseline in Q6. With the Fed locked, the output gap reaches −1.54% in Q9 and unemployment peaks 0.86 pp higher in Q11, with about 1.6 million fewer payroll jobs, more than twice the job loss of the responsive case. The wealth effect on consumption is the largest single channel in both regimes, between two and a half and three times the direct contribution of AI capex once import leakage is netted out.

The response is not linear in the shock. A labour-hoarding threshold makes the marginal unemployment cost of each further 10 points of repricing more than double between small and large shocks. In the locked regime a repricing of about 29% is enough to raise unemployment by 1 pp; the responsive regime needs about 49%. The parameters that matter most are the size of the wealth effect and the two hoarding parameters, not the elasticity of AI capex.

Keywords: AI investment, stress testing, wealth effect, financial accelerator, labour hoarding, monetary policy constraint, impulse response, reverse stress test

Part One · Context & Framework

1.1 Why AI Cooling Is a Macro Question

By late 2026 AI investment has become load-bearing for U.S. growth. In the advance estimate for 2026Q3, real GDP grew at a 3.4% annual rate, and information-processing equipment and software alone added 1.09 percentage points, about a third of the total, before counting data-centre structures. The five largest hyperscalers are on track to spend roughly $0.8 trillion on capital expenditure this year and have guided to more in 2027.

At the same time, the policy backdrop has turned. The war with Iran that began in February pushed energy prices sharply higher; headline CPI rose 3.7% over the year to September while core CPI rose 2.6%. The Federal Reserve raised its target range by 25 bp to 3.75–4.00% in September, its first hike since 2023, and held in October with markets pricing a further hike. The labour market is in a low-hire, low-fire state: October payrolls rose 165,000 and unemployment was 4.1%, but the twelve-month average gain was only 48,000.

Two features of this backdrop make an AI-cooling scenario worth studying as a stress test rather than as a sector story. First, AI capex supports growth far more than it supports employment, so the aggregate statistics may overstate how much the rest of the economy can absorb. Second, a central bank held up by energy inflation cannot use its usual cushion. The day before this note was drafted, the Nasdaq 100 had its worst session since March 2020, which is a reminder that the repricing need not be hypothetical.

1.2 Two Economies in One Aggregate

The aggregate indicators are not mismeasured; they measure a mixture. Three points from first principles matter for what follows.

  • Gross spending is not a GDP contribution. From $Y=C+I+G+X-M$, imported GPUs and servers raise $I$ and $M$ together. The domestic contribution of AI capex is its spending times $(1-m)$, where $m$ is the import share. Headline estimates of AI's share of growth range from about a quarter to three quarters mainly because they treat imports and scope differently.
  • Growth contributions come from changes. Investment adds to GDP growth through its change, not its level. If AI capex merely stops rising, its contribution to growth falls to zero without a single layoff.
  • The labour content is low. A data centre needs construction workers and electricians while it is built and few people after. Payroll data therefore reflect the non-AI economy more cleanly than GDP does, and the gap between strong GDP and weak hiring over the past year is itself a signal of the two-economy split.

The implication for stress testing is that the shock should be traced through the channels by which AI valuations reach labour-intensive spending, rather than read off the AI capex line.

1.3 The Transmission System and Its Loop Gain

Figure 1 maps the whole system before the equations formalise each arrow. Three exogenous shocks enter at the top. Two of them act on AI valuations; the energy shock acts on the policy rule. Valuations reach aggregate demand through four channels, aggregate demand reaches the labour market, and two loops close the system: earnings feedback, which amplifies the shock, and the policy response, which offsets it unless energy inflation pins the policy rate.

Write hats for percentage deviations from a no-shock baseline. Three exogenous drivers enter: a downgrade of expected AI revenue growth $\hat g^e$, a rise in the equity risk premium $\Delta\rho$, and an energy shock $\Delta E$ that acts on policy.

Valuation (DCF with equity duration)

$$ V=\frac{D_{t+1}}{r+\rho-g}\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \hat v = \mathcal D\,(\hat g^e-\Delta r-\Delta\rho) + \zeta\,\hat y,\qquad \mathcal D=\frac{1}{r+\rho-g} $$

$\mathcal D$ is equity duration, large for long-dated AI growth claims. $\zeta\hat y$ is earnings feedback, and it includes a reflexive element specific to AI: one firm's capex is another firm's revenue.

AI Capex, Wealth Effect, Credit Channel

$$ \frac{\Delta I^{AI}}{Y}=s_I\,\phi\,\hat v,\qquad \frac{\Delta C^{W}}{Y}=\mu\,w\,\hat v,\qquad \Delta s=-\eta\,\hat v,\qquad \frac{\Delta I^{O}}{Y}=-\chi\,\Delta s $$

$s_I$ is AI capex as a share of GDP and $\phi$ its elasticity to valuation (Tobin's $q$); $w$ is AI-linked equity wealth relative to GDP and $\mu$ the marginal propensity to consume out of it; $\eta$ maps equity losses into credit spreads, in the spirit of a Merton distance-to-default argument; $\chi$ is the sensitivity of non-AI investment to spreads.

Aggregate Demand and the Policy Constraint

$$ \hat y = A\,\hat v,\qquad A\equiv(1-m)\,s_I\phi+\mu w+\chi\eta $$

$$ \pi=\pi^e+\kappa\hat y+\alpha\,\Delta E,\qquad \Delta r=\underbrace{(\phi_\pi\kappa+\phi_y)}_{\equiv\,\Phi}\,\hat y+\phi_\pi\alpha\,\Delta E $$

Substituting the policy rule into the valuation equation and the result into aggregate demand gives the closed form

$$ \hat y=\frac{A\,\mathcal D\,\big(\hat g^e-\Delta\rho-\phi_\pi\alpha\,\Delta E\big)}{1-G},\qquad G\equiv A\,(\zeta-\mathcal D\,\Phi). $$

The numerator collects the three shocks; the energy shock adds to the AI shocks because it forces the policy rate up. The loop gain $G$ has an amplifying part $A\zeta$ (valuation down, output down, earnings down, valuation down) and a stabilising part $-A\mathcal D\Phi$ (output down, rates down, valuation up). Long equity duration makes the stabiliser stronger, because rate cuts support long-dated claims more. The system is stable for $G<1$.

What "Higher for Longer" Means in the Formula

A central bank that cannot cut because of energy inflation corresponds to $\Phi\to0$. Then $G\to A\zeta$: the only stabiliser in the loop is removed, while $\Delta E$ still pushes in the same direction as the AI shock. This is the mechanism the dynamic model in Part Two quantifies.

1.4 The Labour Block

Labour demand weights each demand component by its labour intensity, adds a structural substitution term, and allows a threshold in firms' willingness to shed hoarded labour.

$$ \hat L=\lambda_C\frac{\Delta C}{Y}+\lambda_I(1-m)\frac{\Delta I^{AI}}{Y}+\lambda_O\frac{\Delta I^O}{Y}+\lambda_G\frac{\Delta G}{Y}-\hat L^{sub},\qquad \lambda_C>\lambda_O>\lambda_I $$

  • Substitution. If capacity was overbuilt, the price of compute falls: $\Delta\ln p^{AI}=-\omega(\hat k^{AI}-\hat d^{AI})$. Cheaper AI and margin pressure both raise substitution of entry-level white-collar work, $\hat L^{sub}=\sigma|\Delta\ln p^{AI}|$ with $\sigma=\sigma_0+\sigma_1\Delta r$. Cooling AI investment and faster AI adoption can therefore move together.
  • Hoarding threshold. After the hiring difficulties of 2021–22, firms hold on to staff through small shortfalls and release them when the expected demand gap exceeds a threshold $\bar\tau$: $\hat L_t=\lambda\hat y_t-\psi\max(0,-\hat y^e_t-\bar\tau)$.
  • Labour supply. $\Delta u\approx-(\hat L-n)$. Slow labour-force growth (low immigration, ageing) lowers the payroll gain needed to hold unemployment steady, which softens the unemployment response for a given loss of jobs.

Part Two · Dynamic Model & Scenario

2.1 From the Static System to a Quarterly Model

The static system has no timing, and timing is what a stress test reports. The quarterly model keeps the same blocks and lets each demand component adjust partially toward its target,

$$ x_t = x_{t-1}+a_x\,(x^*_t-x_{t-1}), $$

so that capex responds with a lag ($a_I=0.35$), the wealth effect builds over several quarters ($a_C=0.30$), and the credit and rate channels are slower still. All variables are deviations from a baseline in which nothing happens.

Valuation and Spreads

$$ v_t = S_t+\zeta\,y_{t-1}-D_{\text{eff}}\,r_t,\qquad \Delta s_t=-\eta\,v_t $$

$S_t$ is the exogenous repricing. $D_{\text{eff}}$ combines equity duration with the pass-through from the policy rate to long rates, so a 1 pp cut lifts AI equities by about 7.5%.

Demand Targets (percent of GDP)

$$ I^{AI*}_t=\tfrac{s_I\phi}{100}v_t,\quad C^{W*}_t=\mu w\,v_t,\quad I^{O*}_t=-\tfrac{\chi}{100}\Delta s_t,\quad D^{R*}_t=-\theta_r r_t,\quad G^*_t=\tau\,v_{t-4},\quad C^{inc}_t=\kappa_c\,y_{t-1} $$

$$ y_t=(1-m)I^{AI}_t+C^W_t+C^{inc}_t+I^O_t+D^R_t+G_t $$

Inflation and the Two Policy Regimes

$$ \pi_t=0.75\,\pi_{t-1}+\kappa\,y_t $$

$$ \text{Fed responds: } r_t=\min\!\big\{0,\;\rho_r r_{t-1}+(1-\rho_r)(\phi_\pi\pi_{t-1}+\phi_y y_{t-1})\big\},\qquad \text{Fed locked: } r_t=0 $$

In the locked regime the policy rate stays on its (already restrictive) baseline path because energy inflation rules out easing. The cap at zero in the responsive regime keeps the comparison about easing room, not about hikes.

Labour Market

$$ L^*_t=h\big[\lambda_C(C^W_t+C^{inc}_t+G_t)+\lambda_I(1-m)I^{AI}_t+\lambda_O(I^O_t+D^R_t)\big]+\psi\min(0,\;y_t+\bar\tau) $$

$$ L^c_t=L^c_{t-1}+a_L(L^*_t-L^c_{t-1}),\qquad L^{sub}_t=-(\sigma_0+\sigma_1 r_t)(1-0.8^t),\qquad L_t=L^c_t+L^{sub}_t,\qquad u_t=-0.85\,L_t $$

$h<1$ scales down the employment response while firms hoard labour; $\psi$ adds separations once the output gap passes $-\bar\tau$. The factor 0.85 lets part of the job loss show up as lower participation rather than higher unemployment.

2.2 Scenario Design and Parameters

The scenario is specified as a market shock, the way supervisory scenarios specify an equity path, rather than through $\hat g^e$ and $\Delta\rho$. AI-linked equities reprice 15% in Q0 and 25% by Q1; the exogenous component then decays at 3% a quarter, a persistent repricing rather than a dip. The endogenous terms (earnings feedback and the rate effect) move the realised path around this. The horizon is 16 quarters.

Three features stand out. The regimes are almost identical for the first three quarters, because the policy rule acts on lagged data and the rate channel is slow. They then separate sharply. With the Fed easing by about 1 pp, AI equities recover to −11% by Q15, rate-sensitive demand turns positive and the output gap closes to −0.35%. With the Fed locked, equities are still near −19% at Q15 and the output gap is still about −1.4%.

Second, the locked regime is not only deeper but slower. Unemployment peaks five quarters later and, four years out, is still 0.82 pp above baseline against 0.25 pp in the responsive case. Third, the inflation response is small in both cases (−0.33 and −0.58 pp). An AI shock of this size is disinflationary, but not by enough to free a central bank facing a 20% rise in energy prices, which is the assumption behind the locked regime.

The wealth effect is the largest single channel in both regimes, between two and a half and three times the direct contribution of AI capex. This follows from the structure rather than from a fine choice of parameters: the AI capex line enters at $(1-m)s_I\phi\approx1.1\%$ of the equity shock, the wealth line at $\mu w=3\%$. Credit is the second channel, and it is the one through which an AI repricing reaches firms that never bought a GPU.

The responsive regime differs in two places. Rate-sensitive demand (housing and other interest-sensitive spending) turns positive from Q2 and reaches about +0.4% of GDP by Q12, and every other channel is smaller because lower rates support the equity price that drives them. Both of these offsets are absent when the Fed is locked.

2.5 Cyclical Job Loss and AI Substitution

The cyclical part follows demand and starts to recover once the output gap turns. The substitution part does not: it builds as overcapacity forms and keeps deepening through Q15. At the end of the horizon it is −0.12% of payrolls in the responsive regime and −0.19% in the locked regime, about 300,000 jobs. Its share of total job loss therefore rises over time, from almost nothing in the first year to about 40% of the remaining shortfall at Q15 in the responsive case.

The term is larger in the locked regime because of $\sigma_1 r_t$: when rates do not fall, the margin pressure that pushes firms to replace labour with cheaper compute stays on. This is the mechanism by which an investment bust and continued AI diffusion hit white-collar employment at the same time.

2.6 Parameter Uncertainty and Sensitivity

Across the 2,000 draws, the locked-regime unemployment peak has a median of +0.84 pp and a P10–P90 range of +0.51 to +1.34 pp. The width of this range, 0.83 pp from parameter uncertainty alone, is larger than the 0.47 pp gap between the two policy regimes at their central values. Figure 5 shows which parameters produce it.

  • The wealth effect dominates. $\mu$ and $w$ enter only as a product, so they have identical bars; moving either by ±40% moves the peak between +0.58 and +1.17 pp.
  • The hoarding parameters come next. The hoarding scale $h$ (+0.67 to +1.06 pp) and the threshold $\bar\tau$ (+0.70 to +1.03 pp, inversely) matter more than the labour intensity of consumption.
  • AI capex parameters matter little. The capex elasticity $\phi$ and the import share $m$ are in the bottom half of the ranking.
  • Policy parameters drop out. $D_{\text{eff}}$ and $\theta_r$ have no effect in the locked regime because the rate does not move. Their absence is the regime assumption showing up in the sensitivity analysis.

For calibration effort, the ranking says where to spend it: on the consumption response to AI-linked wealth, which depends on how concentrated that wealth is, and on firms' hoarding behaviour. It is not where most public discussion of AI cooling focuses, which is the capex line.

2.7 Shock Size, Nonlinearity and a Reverse Stress Test

Figure 6 repeats the simulation for repricings from 5% to 60%. The dots mark the shock size at which the output gap first crosses the hoarding threshold.

Below the threshold, each further 10 points of repricing adds about 0.09 pp to peak unemployment in the responsive regime and 0.19 pp in the locked regime. Between 40% and 50% the increments are 0.26 and 0.45 pp. The threshold is crossed at a 17% repricing when the Fed is locked and at 24% when it can respond, so the 25% scenario sits just past the kink in the responsive case and well past it in the locked case.

Read as a reverse stress test, the table says that with the Fed locked, a repricing about the size of an ordinary bear market in growth stocks is enough to raise unemployment by a full point. With the Fed free to respond, the same outcome needs a fall close to half, comparable to the early stages of the 2000–02 Nasdaq decline. The answer depends heavily on where $\bar\tau$ sits, which is why the threshold, rather than the linear coefficients, is the parameter a reverse stress test should try to pin down.

Part Three · Findings, Implications & Limitations

3.1 What the Responses Say

Policy room is the largest single factor. The same repricing produces a job loss more than twice as large, and a peak five quarters later, when the central bank cannot ease. In the language of Section 1.3, the locked regime removes the stabilising term $-A\mathcal D\Phi$ from the loop gain. In the dynamic model this works mainly through the equity price: rate cuts support long-duration AI claims, which in turn support the wealth and credit channels.

The shock reaches workers through consumption, not through data centres. The direct capex channel is small after import leakage and carries little labour. Most of the job loss comes from the wealth effect and from induced consumption in labour-intensive services. This is consistent with the K-shaped pattern in which AI-linked wealth supports spending by higher-income households.

The labour response is nonlinear and delayed. In the low-hire, low-fire state, unemployment barely moves at first. Once the output gap passes the hoarding threshold, the cost of each additional point of repricing roughly doubles. In the locked regime unemployment keeps rising for two quarters after the output gap has bottomed, and four years out it has recovered only 0.04 pp from its peak.

AI substitution persists after the cycle. A part of the job loss is structural and does not recover with demand. Higher rates make it larger.

None of this establishes how large these effects are in the data. The parameters were chosen, not estimated, and the conclusions above are about structure and ranking. The sensitivity analysis suggests the ranking is more robust than the levels: the wealth channel remains the largest contributor at the output-gap trough under every one-at-a-time perturbation behind Figure 5, in both regimes.

3.2 Implications for Credit Stress Testing

The framework produces internally consistent paths for equities, spreads, output, the policy rate and unemployment, which is what a scenario needs before it is passed to credit models. Three uses follow.

  • Sector PDs. The output gap and spread paths can drive a single-factor (Vasicek) mapping, $PD^{stress}_j=\Phi\!\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(PD_j)-\sqrt{\rho_j}\,Z}{\sqrt{1-\rho_j}}\right)$ with $Z=f(\hat y,\Delta u,\Delta s)$, with sector loadings that differ between the AI supply chain, data-centre developers, early-stage technology and high-end consumer services, and the rest of the book.
  • Listed counterparties. The repricing can enter counterparty PDs directly through distance to default, without passing through macro variables.
  • Wrong-way risk. Typical cases are total-return swaps in which a fund receives AI equity performance (exposure to the fund rises as AI equities fall, while the fund's own credit weakens), data-centre project and private-credit lending where collateral (fast-depreciating GPUs and specialised buildings) and borrower quality deteriorate together, and concentration in a few large technology names as both trading counterparties and bond issuers. PFE for such exposures needs the market and credit factors to move jointly under the scenario.

The loop gain $G$ and the distance of the current output gap from $\bar\tau$ can also be tracked as standing indicators of fragility between scenario runs: $G$ rises with market concentration ($w$), with the sensitivity of spreads to equity ($\eta$), and as the policy constraint ($\Phi\to0$) tightens.

3.3 Limitations

  • Illustrative parameters. Nothing is estimated. The figures show structure and relative size; the levels should not be read as forecasts.
  • Reduced form. The model is partial-equilibrium and backward-looking. It has no forward-looking expectations, no supply side and no feedback from the countries that absorb the import leakage, and it is subject to the Lucas critique under large shocks.
  • Linear blocks with one kink. In a real stress the spread sensitivity $\eta$ itself would rise; here it is constant. The hoarding threshold is the only nonlinearity.
  • A strong regime assumption. The locked regime holds the policy rate for four years. In practice, a demand shock of this size would eventually give the central bank room as energy inflation fades. The locked path is a bound, not a central case.
  • The spread mapping is calibrated, not estimated. The companion credit-channel note found a leverage-to-spread elasticity of about 0.013, far below the textbook accelerator. The equity-to-spread mapping $\eta$ used here is a different object, but it deserves the same empirical treatment.
  • Parameter uncertainty only. The Monte Carlo varies parameters within a fixed model. It says nothing about model uncertainty, which is likely larger.

3.4 Next Steps

  • Estimate the wealth-effect and hoarding parameters, the two that drive most of the uncertainty, using the distribution of equity wealth by income and the behaviour of layoffs relative to output gaps in past downturns.
  • Replace the fixed $\eta$ with a state-dependent spread mapping, using the credit regime estimated in the credit-channel module.
  • Add a severely adverse scenario (a 45% repricing with a crisis-level spread sensitivity) and back-test the model against 2000–02 and the 2022 growth-stock drawdown.
  • Connect the output to sector PDs and to PFE with wrong-way dependence, as outlined in Section 3.2.

T = 16  # quarters

BASE = dict(
    # valuation
    zeta=2.0,        # % equity per 1% output gap (earnings feedback)
    D_eff=7.5,       # % equity per 1pp policy-rate change (duration x long-rate pass-through)
    shock=-25.0,     # exogenous AI equity repricing, %
    shock_persist=0.97,
    # AI capex (q-theory)
    s_I=3.0,         # AI capex, % of GDP
    phi=0.6,         # capex elasticity to valuation
    m=0.4,           # import leakage
    adj_I=0.35,
    # wealth effect
    mu=0.03,         # MPC out of equity wealth
    w=1.0,           # AI-linked equity wealth / GDP
    adj_C=0.30,
    # credit channel
    eta=3.0,         # bp spread per 1% equity drop
    chi=0.5,         # % GDP non-AI investment per 100bp
    adj_O=0.25,
    # rate-sensitive demand
    theta_r=0.5,     # % GDP per 100bp
    adj_R=0.20,
    # fiscal (capital gains tax), lag 4q
    tau=0.002,       # % GDP per 1% equity
    adj_G=0.25,
    # induced consumption
    kappa_c=0.15,
    # inflation & policy
    pi_persist=0.75,
    kappa_pi=0.10,
    rho_r=0.75,
    phi_pi=1.5,
    phi_y=0.8,
    # labor
    lam_C=0.8, lam_O=0.5, lam_I=0.2,
    hoard=0.6,       # employment elasticity scaling while firms hoard labor
    tau_bar=1.0,     # output-gap threshold (%) beyond which hoarded labor is released
    psi=0.5,         # extra separations per 1% gap beyond threshold
    adj_L=0.40,
    sub0=0.20,       # AI substitution: % employment at full overcapacity
    sub1=0.10,       # extra substitution per 1pp higher policy rate
    okun_u=0.85,     # unemployment pp per 1% employment loss (rest = participation drop)
)


def simulate(p, regime="fed_locked", T=T):
    keys = ["shock_v", "v", "y", "iai", "cw", "cinc", "io", "dr", "g", "s", "r", "pi", "L", "L_sub", "u"]
    x = {k: np.zeros(T) for k in keys}
    for t in range(T):
        prev = lambda k: x[k][t - 1] if t > 0 else 0.0
        # exogenous repricing path: half in Q0, full by Q1, then slow decay
        if t == 0:
            sv = 0.6 * p["shock"]
        elif t == 1:
            sv = p["shock"]
        else:
            sv = prev("shock_v") * p["shock_persist"]
        x["shock_v"][t] = sv

        # policy rate (uses last quarter's state)
        if regime == "fed_responds":
            target = p["phi_pi"] * prev("pi") + p["phi_y"] * prev("y")
            r = p["rho_r"] * prev("r") + (1 - p["rho_r"]) * target
            r = min(r, 0.0)  # scenario is about easing room; no hikes from this shock
        else:
            r = 0.0
        x["r"][t] = r

        # valuation: exogenous + earnings feedback + discount-rate effect
        v = sv + p["zeta"] * prev("y") - p["D_eff"] * r
        x["v"][t] = v

        # credit spread (Merton-style link)
        s = -p["eta"] * v
        x["s"][t] = s

        # partial-adjustment demand blocks (% of GDP)
        iai_t = p["s_I"] * p["phi"] * v / 100
        x["iai"][t] = prev("iai") + p["adj_I"] * (iai_t - prev("iai"))
        cw_t = p["mu"] * p["w"] * v
        x["cw"][t] = prev("cw") + p["adj_C"] * (cw_t - prev("cw"))
        io_t = -p["chi"] * s / 100
        x["io"][t] = prev("io") + p["adj_O"] * (io_t - prev("io"))
        dr_t = -p["theta_r"] * r
        x["dr"][t] = prev("dr") + p["adj_R"] * (dr_t - prev("dr"))
        g_t = p["tau"] * x["v"][t - 4] if t >= 4 else 0.0
        x["g"][t] = prev("g") + p["adj_G"] * (g_t - prev("g"))
        x["cinc"][t] = p["kappa_c"] * prev("y")

        y = (1 - p["m"]) * x["iai"][t] + x["cw"][t] + x["cinc"][t] + x["io"][t] + x["dr"][t] + x["g"][t]
        x["y"][t] = y

        # inflation
        x["pi"][t] = p["pi_persist"] * prev("pi") + p["kappa_pi"] * y

        # labor demand
        lab = (p["lam_C"] * (x["cw"][t] + x["cinc"][t] + x["g"][t])
               + p["lam_I"] * (1 - p["m"]) * x["iai"][t]
               + p["lam_O"] * (x["io"][t] + x["dr"][t]))
        L_target = p["hoard"] * lab + p["psi"] * min(0.0, y + p["tau_bar"])
        # AI substitution: overcapacity builds after capex is cut; stronger when rates stay high
        over = 1 - 0.8 ** t
        sub = (p["sub0"] + p["sub1"] * r) * over
        x["L_sub"][t] = -max(sub, 0.0)
        Lc_prev = prev("L") - prev("L_sub")          # cyclical employment component
        Lc = Lc_prev + p["adj_L"] * (L_target - Lc_prev)
        L = Lc + x["L_sub"][t]
        x["L"][t] = L
        x["u"][t] = -p["okun_u"] * L
    return x


def decompose(x, p):
    return {
        "AI capex (net of imports)": ((1 - p["m"]) * x["iai"]).tolist(),
        "Wealth-effect consumption": x["cw"].tolist(),
        "Induced consumption": x["cinc"].tolist(),
        "Credit / non-AI investment": x["io"].tolist(),
        "Rate-sensitive demand": x["dr"].tolist(),
        "State & local spending": x["g"].tolist(),
    }


def monte_carlo(regime, n=2000, spread=0.4, seed=7):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    vary = ["zeta", "D_eff", "phi", "m", "mu", "w", "eta", "chi", "theta_r", "kappa_c",
            "lam_C", "lam_O", "hoard", "tau_bar", "psi", "sub0"]
    out = {k: [] for k in ["y", "u", "v", "s"]}
    for _ in range(n):
        p = dict(BASE)
        for k in vary:
            p[k] = BASE[k] * rng.uniform(1 - spread, 1 + spread)
        p["m"] = min(p["m"], 0.8)
        x = simulate(p, regime)
        for k in out:
            out[k].append(x[k])
    bands = {}
    for k, arr in out.items():
        a = np.array(arr)
        bands[k] = {q: np.percentile(a, q, axis=0).round(3).tolist() for q in (10, 50, 90)}
    return bands


def summary(x):
    def peak(k, fn=np.min):
        arr = x[k]
        i = int(np.argmin(arr) if fn is np.min else np.argmax(arr))
        return round(float(arr[i]), 2), i
    return {
        "AI equity trough (%)": peak("v"),
        "Output gap trough (% GDP)": peak("y"),
        "Credit spread peak (bp)": peak("s", np.max),
        "Policy rate (pp, trough)": peak("r"),
        "Core inflation trough (pp)": peak("pi"),
        "Employment trough (%)": peak("L"),
        "Unemployment peak (pp)": peak("u", np.max),
        "Jobs lost at trough (thousands)": (round(float(np.min(x["L"])) / 100 * 160000), int(np.argmin(x["L"]))),
    }


if __name__ == "__main__":
    res = {}
    for reg in ("fed_responds", "fed_locked"):
        x = simulate(BASE, reg)
        res[reg] = {
            "path": {k: np.round(v, 3).tolist() for k, v in x.items()},
            "decomp": {k: np.round(v, 3).tolist() for k, v in decompose(x, BASE).items()},
            "summary": summary(x),
            "bands": monte_carlo(reg),
        }
    res["params"] = BASE
    with open("irf_results.json", "w") as f:
        json.dump(res, f, ensure_ascii=False, indent=1)
    for reg in ("fed_responds", "fed_locked"):
        print(reg)
        for k, v in res[reg]["summary"].items():
            print(f"  {k:36s} {v[0]:>9}  @Q{v[1]}")
