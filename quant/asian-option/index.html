Pricing and Exposure of Arithmetic Asian Options: Closed-Form Geometric Anchor, Control-Variate Monte Carlo, and American Monte Carlo PFE

亚洲期权定价与暴露计算：几何平均闭式解、方差控制蒙特卡洛与美式蒙特卡洛PFE

✍ Dong Liu 📅 August 2026 🔗 LinkedIn 💬 Comments welcome

Abstract

This note is in two parts. Part One treats the pricing problem: the closed-form value of the continuously-monitored geometric-average Asian option is derived from the law of $\int_0^T W_u\,du$ under the risk-neutral measure $Q$, then used as an exact control variate for the arithmetic-average Asian option, which admits no closed form because a sum of correlated lognormals is not itself lognormal. A worked example on $N=20{,}000$ simulated paths shows a variance-reduction factor of roughly $10^{-3}$ relative to naive Monte Carlo.

Part Two treats the exposure problem: computing a Potential Future Exposure (PFE) profile for a path-dependent claim by nested simulation costs $O(N\cdot T\cdot M)$, which is infeasible at realistic scale. An algebraic reduction of the residual Asian payoff to an equivalent option with an adjusted strike is derived and paired with a regression-based (American Monte Carlo) estimator of the $t_k$-conditional risk-neutral value, reducing the cost to a single forward simulation plus one cross-sectional regression per exposure date. The illustration uses a one-year Asian option monitored weekly ($n=52$, $N=1{,}000$ paths), reporting the resulting EE and $\mathrm{PFE}_{99}$ profiles.

Both parts are self-contained; Part Two's regression machinery does not require re-deriving Part One's closed form, but reuses its control-variate identity as the natural refinement noted in §5.3. Python code for both parts is given in the appendices.

Keywords: Asian options, geometric average, control variates, American Monte Carlo, Longstaff–Schwartz regression, Potential Future Exposure, counterparty credit risk

Part One · Pricing

1. Measure-Theoretic Preliminaries and the Geometric-Average Closed Form

1.1 The Risk-Neutral Dynamics

Fix a filtered probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal F, (\mathcal F_t)_{t \ge 0}, Q)$ satisfying the usual conditions, where $Q$ is the equivalent martingale measure associated with the money-market numeraire $B_t = e^{rt}$, $r$ constant. Under $Q$ the underlying follows geometric Brownian motion, $$ dS_t = r S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t^{Q}, \qquad S_0 \text{ given}, $$ with $W^Q$ a standard $Q$-Brownian motion, so that $$ S_t = S_0 \exp\!\left( \left(r - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma W_t^Q \right). $$ Every object below is a functional of this single process: the geometric average, the arithmetic average, and, in Part Two, the conditional exposure at an intermediate date. Every price is a $Q$-expectation of a discounted functional. The one place a second measure enters (the outer scenario distribution for PFE) is flagged explicitly in §3.1.

1.2 The Law of the Continuous Geometric Average

Define $G_T = \exp\!\left(\frac1T \int_0^T \ln S_u \, du\right)$. Substituting the explicit form of $\ln S_u$, $$ \ln G_T = \ln S_0 + \left(r - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)\frac{T}{2} + \frac{\sigma}{T}\int_0^T W_u^Q \, du. $$ The only stochastic term is $\int_0^T W_u^Q\,du$, a centered Gaussian functional of Brownian motion. By Fubini and $\mathbb E^Q[W_sW_t]=s\wedge t$, $$ \mathrm{Var}\!\left(\int_0^T W_u^Q\,du\right) = \int_0^T\!\!\int_0^T (s \wedge t) \, ds\, dt = \frac{T^3}{3}. $$ Hence $\ln G_T \sim N(m, v)$ under $Q$ with $$ m = \ln S_0 + \left(r-\tfrac12\sigma^2\right)\frac{T}{2}, \qquad v = \frac{\sigma^2 T}{3}. $$ This is the entire content of the geometric simplification: averaging inside the exponent is linear in a Gaussian process, so the average stays lognormal. That linearity is destroyed the moment one averages $S_u$ itself. This is the obstruction taken up in §2.

1.3 Closed-Form Valuation

Definition 1: Geometric-Average Asian Call

Pays $(G_T - K)^+$ at $T$, where $G_T = \exp\left(\frac1T\int_0^T \ln S_u\,du\right)$.

Proposition 1: Closed-Form Price

$$ V_{\text{geo}} = e^{-rT}\left[e^{m+\frac12 v}\,\Phi(d_1) - K\,\Phi(d_2)\right], \qquad d_1 = \frac{m+v-\ln K}{\sqrt v},\ \ d_2 = d_1 - \sqrt v, $$ with $m,v$ as in §1.2 and $\Phi$ the standard normal CDF.

Since $\ln G_T\sim N(m,v)$, $\mathbb E^Q[(G_T-K)^+]$ is the standard lognormal call expectation evaluated at "spot" $e^m$ and "variance" $v$. The closed form is due to Kemna and Vorst (1990) and is reproduced here from the Brownian covariance identity, since the derivation is what makes the arithmetic case's failure transparent rather than mysterious. In discretely-monitored form ($t_1\lt\cdots\lt t_n=T$), the same argument holds with $\ln G_T=\frac1n\sum_i \ln S_{t_i}$ and $v$ the exact finite-sum variance $\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i,j}\sigma^2(t_i\wedge t_j)$, converging to $\sigma^2T/3$ for fine monitoring grids; the continuous formula is used as the analytic anchor throughout.

2. Arithmetic-Average Pricing via Control-Variate Monte Carlo

2.1 Why There Is No Closed Form

Define $A_T = \frac1T\int_0^T S_u\,du$ (or its discrete analogue). Unlike $\ln G_T$, $A_T$ is a linear combination of lognormal $S_u$, and a sum of correlated lognormals is not itself lognormal, so no analogue of Proposition 1 exists for $A_T$. What is available is an exact pathwise ordering:

Corollary 1: Pathwise and Price Ordering (AM-GM)

$A_T \ge G_T$ $Q$-a.s., and since $x\mapsto(x-K)^+$ is nondecreasing, $(A_T-K)^+\ge(G_T-K)^+$ pathwise; hence $V_{\text{arith}}\ge V_{\text{geo}}$.

$A_T$ and $G_T$ are close to comonotonic, since their pathwise gap is driven by the same realized-path variance, so their discounted payoffs are highly correlated on the same simulated path. This is precisely the setting in which control variates are efficient.

2.2 The Optimal Control-Variate Coefficient

Let $X=e^{-rT}(A_T-K)^+$ and $Y=e^{-rT}(G_T-K)^+$ on the same path, with $\mu_Y=V_{\text{geo}}$ known exactly.

Proposition 2: Variance-Minimizing Control Variate

$$ \hat X(\beta) = \bar X - \beta(\bar Y - \mu_Y) $$ is unbiased for every $\beta$; $\mathrm{Var}(\hat X(\beta))$ is minimized at $\beta^\star=\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{Var}(Y)$, giving $\mathrm{Var}(\hat X(\beta^\star))=\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)(1-\rho^2)$, $\rho=\mathrm{Corr}(X,Y)$.

The proof is the one-dimensional least-squares argument: differentiate the quadratic in $\beta$, set to zero. Note the form of the result: $\beta^\star$ is exactly the OLS slope of $X$ on $Y$, and $\hat X(\beta^\star)$ is the OLS residual of $X$ against $Y$, recentered at the known mean $\mu_Y$. Control-variate correction and regression against a correlated, analytically tractable proxy are the same operation. Part Two's AMC estimator (§4.3) is this identity generalized to a cross-sectional basis rather than a single scalar $Y$.

2.3 Worked Example

Take $S_0=K=100$, $r=1\%$, $\sigma=25\%$, $T=1$, with $N=20{,}000$ simulated paths on a daily grid ($n=252$). Figure 1 shows a representative subset of the simulated paths.

The control-variate estimator's variance is roughly $1/900$ of the naive estimator's, i.e. the same standard error would require about $900\times$ as many naive paths. This is Proposition 2 realized numerically, not an approximation: the reduction factor is exactly $1-\rho^2$, and $\rho$ here happens to be very close to $1$ because at-the-money arithmetic and geometric averages over a full year of daily observations are nearly indistinguishable path-by-path.

Part Two · Exposure

3. The Nested-Simulation Problem

A Potential Future Exposure profile requires, at each of $T$ future exposure dates and for each of $N$ scenario paths, the conditional risk-neutral value of the remaining claim. For a path-dependent claim with no closed form, the direct approach re-simulates $M$ inner paths from every $(path, date)$ pair, at total cost $O(N\cdot T\cdot M)$. With $N=1{,}000$, $T=52$ (weekly monitoring, below), and $M=20{,}000$, a modest inner sample size, this is $1.04\times10^9$ path-steps of inner simulation alone, before the outer simulation is even counted. Sections 4–5 replace this with a method whose cost is $O(N\cdot n)$ for the single forward simulation plus $O(n\cdot N\cdot P^2)$ for $n$ regressions of $P$ basis terms each, with no inner simulation at all.

4. The Adjusted-Strike Reduction and the American Monte Carlo Estimator

4.1 The Measure Convention for PFE

PFE is properly a hybrid-measure object: outer scenarios are generated under the physical measure $P$, while the mark-to-market within each scenario is a $Q$-conditional expectation. Conflating the two silently substitutes the risk-neutral drift for the real-world drift. For the numerical illustration in §5, the simplified convention $P=Q$ is adopted, consistent with elementary expositions such as Green (2015, ch. 14); disentangling $P$ from $Q$ (e.g. via a drift-adjusted historical or filtered-historical scenario generator) is a separate methodological layer, orthogonal to the regression machinery below.

4.2 The Adjusted-Strike Reduction

Fix a monitoring grid $0=t_0\lt t_1\lt\cdots\lt t_n=T$ and an exposure date $t_k$. Write $A_{t_k}=\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^k S_{t_i}$ for the realized running average and $\bar S_{k,n}=\frac1{n-k}\sum_{i=k+1}^n S_{t_i}$ for the average of the unrealized observations.

Proposition 3: Residual Payoff as an Adjusted-Strike Asian Option

$$ \left(\frac{k}{n}A_{t_k} + \frac{n-k}{n}\bar S_{k,n} - K\right)^{\!+} = \frac{n-k}{n}\left(\bar S_{k,n} - K'_{t_k}\right)^{\!+}, \qquad K'_{t_k} = \frac{n}{n-k}K - \frac{k}{n-k}A_{t_k}. $$

Conditional on $\mathcal F_{t_k}$, the residual claim is again an arithmetic Asian option on $[t_k,T]$ with a shifted strike. The relevant state collapses from $(S_{t_k},A_{t_k})$ to the single effective moneyness $x_{t_k}=S_{t_k}/K'_{t_k}$, whenever $K'_{t_k}>0$.

Corollary 2: The Degenerate Boundary

If $K'_{t_k}\le 0$ (i.e. $A_{t_k}\ge\frac{n}{k}K$), the residual claim is $Q$-a.s. in the money regardless of the future path, with deterministic value $e^{-r(T-t_k)}\left[\frac{k}{n}A_{t_k}+\frac{n-k}{n}\mathbb E^Q[\bar S_{k,n}\mid\mathcal F_{t_k}]-K\right]$. A regression against $\log x_{t_k}$ that does not isolate this boundary fits paths with $x_{t_k}\to\pm\infty$ and can degrade the design-matrix condition number by many orders of magnitude. This was verified numerically in an earlier iteration of this exercise (condition number $>10^{24}$ unguarded, well-conditioned once isolated).

4.3 The Regression Estimator

Simulate $N$ paths once, in full, to $T$. On path $j$ the terminal payoff $\mathrm{pay}_T^{(j)}$ is already known. Define $Y_k^{(j)}=e^{-r(T-t_k)}\mathrm{pay}_T^{(j)}$ and a finite basis $\{\phi_p\}_{p=0}^{3}$, $\phi_p(x)=(\log x)^p$.

Definition 2: American Monte Carlo / Regress-Now Estimator

$$ \hat\beta_k = \arg\min_{\beta}\ \sum_{j=1}^N \Big(Y_k^{(j)} - \textstyle\sum_p \beta_p\,\phi_p(\log x_{t_k}^{(j)})\Big)^2, \qquad \hat V(t_k,x^{(j)}) = \sum_p \hat\beta_{k,p}\,\phi_p(\log x_{t_k}^{(j)}). $$ $\hat V(t_k,\cdot)$ is a sieve estimator of $\mathbb E^Q[Y_k\mid x_{t_k}]$, in the tradition of Longstaff and Schwartz (2001), adapted from the early-exercise setting to pathwise valuation of a European path-dependent claim: no backward induction over an exercise boundary is needed, and the $n-1$ regressions are mutually independent.

As noted in §2.2, this is the control-variate identity generalized: $\hat V(t_k,\cdot)$ is $\bar Y_k$ corrected by a fitted, basis-projected control, borrowing statistical power across paths sharing similar $x_{t_k}$ rather than requiring fresh simulation from each state. Cost per date is $O(NP^2)$; over the grid, $O(nNP^2)$, against $O(nNM)$ for nested simulation.

At $N=5{,}000$ the picture is unchanged in shape but sharper: $\mathrm{PFE}_{99}$ climbs from 11.0 to a peak of 42.6 around week 32, then eases as the Corollary 2 boundary takes over. By week 51, $2{,}162$ of $5{,}000$ paths (43%) are locked in. EE drifts mildly upward, from 6.03 to a local high of 6.30 near the PFE$_{99}$ peak, before easing back to 6.10.

That drift is real but small, about $0.29$ across the whole horizon, against a PFE$_{99}$ range of $32.3$: a ratio of well under $1\%$. Plotted on a shared axis with PFE$_{99}$, EE would be visually indistinguishable from a flat line; Figure 2 gives it its own axis precisely so that this small but genuine drift is visible rather than compressed to nothing. The near-flatness itself is not a bug: under the risk-neutral scenario measure of §4.1, the discounted price process is a martingale in expectation, so $\mathrm{EE}(t_k)=\mathbb E^Q[\hat V(t_k,\cdot)^+]$ tracks the discounted current price and moves only through the second-order convexity of $\max(\cdot,0)$ and the Corollary 2 lock-in, both small next to the variance $\mathrm{PFE}_{99}$ is measuring. $\mathrm{PFE}_{99}$ (Figure 3), by construction, tracks exactly the accumulating variance EE is designed not to show: it climbs through the first two-thirds of the year as uncertainty in the unrealized average accumulates, peaks near week 32, and thereafter is pulled off its peak by the same lock-in visible in the deep-ITM bar count in Figure 3, even as time-to-maturity mechanically shrinks. This lock-in effect is specific to average-rate claims and has no analogue in a plain-vanilla PFE profile, which is precisely why Corollary 2's explicit accounting, rather than a regression run blindly over the full sample, is not an optional refinement but a required part of the estimator for this product class.

As in Part One, a validation exercise not reproduced in full here cross-checked $\hat V(t_k,\cdot)$ against brute-force nested simulation at sampled interior states; the cubic basis leaves a residual bias on the order of $10$–$20\%$ near the money, consistent with the sieve-estimation diagnosis of §4.3. A production refinement would apply the control-variate identity of §2.2 directly here: regressing $Y_k - Y_k^{\text{geo}}$ rather than $Y_k$, and adding back the exact conditional geometric price from Proposition 1 applied on $[t_k,T]$. This anchors the regression's level and leaves it to fit only the smaller-variance arithmetic-geometric spread.

A.1: Geometric Closed Form & Control-Variate Monte Carlo

Implements Proposition 1 (geometric closed form), the control-variate estimator of Proposition 2, and reproduces Figure 1 and the Section 2.3 table.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from math import log, exp, sqrt, erf

def norm_cdf(x):
    return 0.5*(1+erf(x/sqrt(2.0)))

def geo_asian_closed_price(S0,K,r,sigma,T):
    m = log(S0)+(r-0.5*sigma**2)*T/2.0
    v = sigma**2*T/3.0
    d1=(m+v-log(K))/sqrt(v); d2=d1-sqrt(v)
    return exp(-r*T)*(exp(m+0.5*v)*norm_cdf(d1)-K*norm_cdf(d2))

S0,K,r,sigma,T = 100.0,100.0,0.01,0.25,1.0
n_steps, N = 252, 20000
rng = np.random.default_rng(12345)
dt = T/n_steps
drift=(r-0.5*sigma**2)*dt; vol=sigma*sqrt(dt)
increments = drift+vol*rng.normal(size=(N,n_steps))
logS = log(S0)+np.cumsum(increments,axis=1)
S = np.empty((N,n_steps+1)); S[:,0]=S0; S[:,1:]=np.exp(logS)

mc_A = np.maximum(S.mean(axis=1)-K,0.0)
mc_G = np.maximum(np.exp(np.log(S[:,1:]).mean(axis=1))-K,0.0)
geo_closed = geo_asian_closed_price(S0,K,r,sigma,T)
disc = exp(-r*T)
mc_A_d, mc_G_d = disc*mc_A, disc*mc_G

cov = np.cov(mc_A_d, mc_G_d, ddof=1)[0,1]
var_g = np.var(mc_G_d, ddof=1)
beta = cov/var_g
naive_price = mc_A_d.mean()
naive_se = mc_A_d.std(ddof=1)/sqrt(N)
cv_price = mc_A_d.mean() - beta*(mc_G_d.mean()-geo_closed)
cv_resid = mc_A_d - beta*mc_G_d
cv_se = cv_resid.std(ddof=1)/sqrt(N)

print(f"geo_closed={geo_closed:.4f}")
print(f"naive_price={naive_price:.4f} se={naive_se:.4f}")
print(f"cv_price={cv_price:.4f} se={cv_se:.4f}")
print(f"variance reduction factor = {(cv_se/naive_se)**2:.4f}")
print(f"beta={beta:.4f}  corr={np.corrcoef(mc_A_d,mc_G_d)[0,1]:.5f}")

# ---- Plot: subset of simulated paths ----
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2,4.2), dpi=110)
t_grid = np.linspace(0,T,n_steps+1)
rng_plot = np.random.default_rng(7)
show_idx = rng_plot.choice(N, 40, replace=False)
for i in show_idx:

A.2: Adjusted-Strike Reduction & American Monte Carlo PFE (N=5,000)

Implements the adjusted-strike reduction of Proposition 3, the Corollary 2 boundary handling, the Definition 2 regression estimator, and reproduces Figures 2–3 and the Section 5 table.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from math import log, exp

S0,K,r,sigma,T = 100.0,100.0,0.01,0.25,1.0
n = 52
N = 5000
dt = T/n

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
drift=(r-0.5*sigma**2)*dt; vol=np.sqrt(dt)*sigma
increments = drift+vol*rng.normal(size=(N,n))
logS = log(S0)+np.cumsum(increments,axis=1)
S = np.empty((N,n+1)); S[:,0]=S0; S[:,1:]=np.exp(logS)
running_sum = np.cumsum(S[:,1:],axis=1)
payoff_T = np.maximum(running_sum[:,-1]/n - K,0.0)

def basis(logx):
    return np.column_stack([logx**p for p in range(4)])

expo_dates_idx = list(range(1,n))
t_list, EE_list, PFE99_list, itm_list = [], [], [], []
for k in expo_dates_idx:
    t_i = k*dt
    A_t = running_sum[:,k-1]/k
    S_t = S[:,k]
    Kp = (n/(n-k))*K - (k/(n-k))*A_t
    deep_itm = Kp<=1e-6
    normal = ~deep_itm
    disc_ti = exp(-r*(T-t_i))
    Y_i = disc_ti*payoff_T
    V_hat = np.empty(N)
    if deep_itm.any():
        idx=np.where(deep_itm)[0]
        remain=n-k
        avg_fwd_factor = np.mean([exp(r*(j+1)*dt) for j in range(remain)]) if remain>0 else 1.0
        avg_fwd = S_t[idx]*avg_fwd_factor
        intrinsic = (k/n)*A_t[idx]+((n-k)/n)*avg_fwd-K
        V_hat[idx]=disc_ti*np.maximum(intrinsic,0.0)
    if normal.any():
        idx=np.where(normal)[0]
        logx=np.log(S_t[idx]/Kp[idx])
        lo,hi=np.quantile(logx,[0.01,0.99]); logx_c=np.clip(logx,lo,hi)
        Phi=basis(logx_c)
        beta,*_=np.linalg.lstsq(Phi,Y_i[idx],rcond=None)
        V_hat[idx]=Phi@beta
    EE=np.mean(np.maximum(V_hat,0.0))
    PFE99=np.quantile(V_hat,0.99)
    t_list.append(t_i); EE_list.append(EE); PFE99_list.append(PFE99); itm_list.append(int(deep_itm.sum()))

t_arr, EE_arr, PFE99_arr, itm_arr = map(np.array, (t_list, EE_list, PFE99_list, itm_list))

# ---- Figure 2: EE(t) alone, own y-scale ----
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.5,4.2), dpi=130)
ax.plot(t_arr, EE_arr, color="#1a3a5c", lw=1.8, marker='o', markersize=3.5)
ax.set_xlabel("t (years)")
ax.set_ylabel("EE(t)")
ax.set_title("Expected Exposure: AMC Regression (N=5000, n=52 weekly)")
ax.spines['top'].set_visible(False)
ax.spines['right'].set_visible(False)
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("/home/claude/figs/ee_profile.png", dpi=130)
print("saved ee_profile.png")

# ---- Figure 3: PFE99(t) + deep-ITM bar chart, dual axis ----
fig, ax1 = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.5,4.2), dpi=130)
ax1.plot(t_arr, PFE99_arr, color="#8a5a00", lw=1.8, marker='o', markersize=3.5, label="PFE$_{99}(t)$")
ax1.set_xlabel("t (years)")
ax1.set_ylabel("PFE$_{99}(t)$", color="#8a5a00")
ax1.tick_params(axis='y', colors="#8a5a00")
ax1.spines['top'].set_visible(False)
ax2 = ax1.twinx()

ax2.set_ylabel("# deep-ITM paths", color="#c0392b")
ax2.tick_params(axis='y', colors="#c0392b")
